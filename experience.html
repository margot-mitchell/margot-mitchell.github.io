P1 · Poldrack Lab

Factor-analytic validation of behavioral task measures (RDoC Aim 1)

Using confirmatory and exploratory methods, do repeated measures of control, attention, and working memory across people support the construct-to-measure mappings stipulated in the RDoC matrix?

Confirmatory/Exploratory FA · Mixed-effects models & ICCs · Evidence accumulation and race models

I joined the lab during the data collection stage of this project: an online battery of , each repeated five times per person, in 500 participants. Since meeting the target N before the grant deadline, I’ve led all analysis and modeling branches. One of our aims was to derive a confirmatory model linking each construct to measures from multiple tasks. The model drew on the original , which proposes task-to-construct mappings, and on a survey of experts who have published extensively on these tasks. My contributions build on this work, which my colleagues presented at OHBM 2024.

I used lavaan in R to test confirmatory factor models against the behavioral data, and we’ve since moved into exploratory analyses: fitting computational models, including diffusion model variants in EMC2, to see how the factor solutions depend on how the measures are parameterized, and looking at practice effects across sessions. This project has motivated me to keep asking construct-validity questions.

What I did

  • I worked with software developers in the lab to resolve issues on the server side that had previously paused data acquisition.
  • I collected data from the remaining 300+ of 500 participants (12 hours of task data each) needed to meet the tight grant deadline, ramping up recruitment while keeping attrition and exclusions low.
  • I built a run-, task-, and participant-level exclusion system to classify degenerate performance and intervene or remove participants in real time.
  • I cleaned the data, including correcting flipped response mappings and running bot-detection checks, and checked for the expected group-level behavioral patterns before modeling.
  • I assessed the internal and external validity of all measures, fit linear mixed-effects models, fit evidence accumulation models, fit confirmatory and exploratory factor analytic models, and ran sensitivity analyses and parameter recovery checks where appropriate.
  • I wrote significant portions of the manuscript.
  • I collaborated with Nilam Ram, training in SEM and multilevel modeling, and learned conflict-task modeling from Andrew Heathcote.

P2 · Poldrack Lab

Dense-sampling task fMRI: acquisition and quality control (RDoC Aim 2)

Do fMRI contrasts organize into a structure similar to the behavioral factors, as we’d expect if they were measuring the same constructs?

Task fMRI · BIDS · Python · Automated QC

Participants come in for a total of 13 sessions and 12 scans. We collect T1- and T2-weighted anatomical scans, diffusion sequences, ~40 minutes of rest-fMRI, and ~900 minutes of task-fMRI across the thirteen tasks. Participants come in once a week, every week, for the task-fMRI sessions. They repeat each task every other week for a total of five times inside the scanner, over the course of the 10 task-fMRI sessions.

Our fMRI task set is very similar to that of the behavioral aim. For some tasks, the trial numbers and timings differ slightly in the fMRI version in order to respect counterbalanced session-length constraints unique to the scanner. There are 13 fMRI tasks (instead of 12) because we run three Span variations (instead of two) to support the full range of fMRI task contrasts we are interested in. The tasks are on GitHub: rdoc-fmri-experiments

What I did

  • I built an automated pipeline for ongoing quality control of in-scanner task performance (rdoc-fmri-behavioral-qc).
  • I built a package for constructing BIDS-compliant event files using the behavioral task data for our 13 cognitive tasks (rdoc-fmri-events). Event files are necessary for linking behavioral and neural events when we fit general linear models.
  • I manage recruitment, scheduling, IRB oversight, salivary hormone sample care and shipping, and data collection, and I deal with issues as they come up. I consistently scan 10–20+ hours a week, on my own.
  • I’ve kept us on track to reach our target sample by the deadline: 45 complete participants by the end of 2026.

P3 · Poldrack Lab x Women’s Brain Health Initiative (WBHI)

Classifier for RDoC × Women’s Brain Health Initiative (WBHI collaboration)

How does the functional connectivity of brain networks change across the menstrual cycle?

Classification (machine learning vs. rule-based) · Hormone simulation · Hormone analysis

In collaboration with the Women’s Brain Health Initiative, we added a hormone component to our dense-sampling fMRI study. Participants give a saliva sample at every session, report birth control use and other women’s health information, and track their periods and sleep. Salivary hormones and self-reported tracking are both noisy, and blood draws weren’t an option, so we need an estimator that gives a best guess of when each participant shifts between the follicular and luteal phases. I analyze the hormone data, and I built that classifier on simulated data.

What I did

  • Built a classification pipeline for predicting shifts in menstrual phase, to be used once data collection ends and we receive the remaining hormone results (start of 2027).
  • Analyzed our hormone results.
  • Reviewed the literature, and found open datasets and the metrics they report.
  • Simulated ground-truth hormone patterns and menstrual cycles, observed salivary hormone levels, self-reported responses to the daily tracking app and weekly surveys, missingness, and scan dates.
  • Tested machine-learning against rule-based approaches.

P4 · Poldrack Lab x Human-Centered Artificial Intelligence (HAI)

Cognitive traits and AI-enhanced work efficiency (HAI collaboration)

Do cognitive traits, as measured via self-report and experimental tasks, predict how individuals perform on coding tasks designed to simulate and mimic daily work tasks?

Data cleaning & scoring · Quality control

In a collaboration with economists at Stanford’s Institute for Human-Centered Artificial Intelligence (HAI), I was asked to evaluate the cognitive measures from a large hackathon experiment they ran with thousands of employees at a large company abroad. The company wants to know whether certain traits predict how people react to using AI at work: whether they adopt it, and how much it improves their performance.

What I did

  • I cleaned and scored the raw data from 6,000 employees on six cognitive measures, several of them new to me.
  • I caught scoring errors in their task code and identified interesting behavioral trends.
  • I returned a fully QC-ed, regression-ready matrix with documentation of each measure and a justification for every QC threshold I recommended.

Effect of AI’s reliability on human response inhibition in a shared control environment

Does manipulating the probability that an AI system engages successfully change the observed impairment to human response inhibition when responsibility is dispersed?

AI impacts · Experiment design · Response inhibition

A 2025 preprint from my colleagues found that human response inhibition was impaired significantly in a shared control environment, as evidenced by a . NIMH · Research Domain Criteria (RDoC)

RDoC is NIMH’s research framework for studying mental health and psychopathology as variation in basic dimensions of brain and behavioral functioning, organized into six domains, rather than starting from symptom-based diagnoses. Researchers study these dimensions from normal to abnormal, across units of analysis (from genes and circuits to behavior and self-report), development, and environment, to inform better assessment, diagnosis, and treatment.
